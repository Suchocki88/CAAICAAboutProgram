About This Program

Understanding AI: A Conceptual and Experiential Approach
Teacher's Implementation Guide

Welcome to Understanding AI

This course represents a fundamentally different approach to teaching machine learning and artificial intelligence. Rather than targeting computer science or advanced math students, Understanding AI brings these critical concepts to social science students through hands-on experimentation, creative application, and critical analysis of AI's societal impact.

Your students won't write code or solve complex equations. Instead, they'll become AI researchers—conducting systematic experiments, documenting observations, and developing the literacy skills needed to navigate an AI-shaped world.

Course Structure Overview

0.5 Credit Semester Course

  • Duration: 16-17 weeks (approximately 88 class days total)
  • Meeting frequency: Daily periods (50 minutes recommended)
  • Credits: Half credit, typically satisfying social studies elective requirements
  • Structure: 8 chapters with 71 core lesson days + built-in flexibility through Spotlights and Extra Project Days

Five Lesson Types

The course alternates between five distinct lesson types, each serving specific pedagogical purposes:

1. Lesson Days (19 total)

Purpose: Introduce foundational concepts and terminology

Structure: Direct instruction, guided discussions, concept exploration

Student role: Active listening, note-taking, concept processing

2. Lab Days (15 total)

Purpose: Hands-on experimentation with Alia (AI system)

Structure: Structured experiments with hypothesis testing

Student role: Active researchers conducting experiments

Key component: Field journal documentation

3. Discussion Days (19 total)

Purpose: Analysis, reflection, synthesis, and peer learning

Structure: Socratic seminars, small group analysis, class debates

Student role: Critical thinkers, discussion participants

4. Project Days (10 total)

Purpose: Apply AI collaboration skills to creative writing

Structure: Structured activities with Alia that build AI writing skills

Student role: Developmental editors directing AI ghost writer

5. Assessment Days (8 total)

Purpose: Evaluate understanding through formal assessments

Structure: RAT (practice exam) + Unit Exam using Pyramid format

Coverage: 4 units, each covering 2 chapters

Extra Project Days

In addition to the 10 formal Project Days, the program includes Extra Project Days where students work solely on their creative writing projects without structured Alia activities.

Why this matters: Formal Project Days include valuable AI skill-building activities, but these can limit the time students spend actually writing. Extra Project Days provide dedicated, uninterrupted time for students to focus on their creative work.

Use Extra Project Days when:

  • Students need more time to develop their stories
  • The project deadline is approaching
  • Students are making strong progress and need momentum
  • You've moved quickly through other content and have time available

Flexibility and Pacing: You Have Breathing Room

Built-In Flexibility

This program is designed as a 71-lesson curriculum with multiple sources of built-in flexibility. This flexibility is intentional and essential.

Sources of Flexibility

  • Spotlight Essays (10 total): Supplemental readings that extend chapter content. These are optional—content from Spotlights does NOT appear on exams. Use them when time permits or assign as enrichment.
  • Extra Project Days: Dedicated time for students to work solely on their creative writing projects without structured Alia activities.
  • Natural pacing variation: Some lessons will move faster than expected, creating additional flex time.

Permission to Adjust Pacing

Quality of learning matters more than speed through curriculum. If a lesson needs two class periods instead of one, take two periods. Skip a Spotlight if time is tight. Add an Extra Project Day if students need more time with their creative work. The flexibility is built in—use it.

When to Use Flexibility

  • Technical issues: If Alia access is interrupted, don't skip the lab—delay it
  • Rich discussion emerges: Students are making connections? Extend the discussion day
  • Complexity appears: Some concepts (like training data or bias) may need extra processing time
  • Assessment prep: Unit exams are conceptually challenging—allow review time
  • Project work: Students need more time on their creative writing? Add an Extra Project Day

About Spotlight Essays

The program includes 10 Spotlight Essays—supplemental readings that extend beyond the core lessons. These include case studies, deep dives, technology insights, and hands-on activities.

Key Points About Spotlights:

  • Not assessed: Spotlight content does NOT appear on unit exams
  • Flexible use: Assign as homework, use during class, or skip entirely based on time
  • Enrichment focus: They add depth and real-world connections without being essential
  • Student interest: Consider letting students choose which Spotlights to explore

Think of Spotlights as side trails that branch off from the main path—valuable for those who want to explore further, but not required for course completion.

About Time Estimates

Time estimates in lesson materials are approximations to help with planning—not rigid requirements.

In Student-Facing Materials:

You'll notice student lessons generally do not include specific time allocations (like "5 minutes" or "20 minutes"). This is intentional. Different classes move at different paces, and we want to avoid creating artificial pressure to "finish on time."

Communicate timing verbally or write it on the board based on your class's needs and your professional judgment.

In Teacher Guides:

Teacher guides DO include suggested time allocations to help you plan your period. However, please note:

  • These estimates are generated by AI without direct classroom experience
  • They're educated guesses, not empirically tested timings
  • Your class may need more or less time based on:
    • Student engagement level
    • Depth of discussion that emerges
    • Technical issues
    • Prior knowledge and experience
    • Class size and dynamics

Your Professional Judgment Matters Most

Trust yourself. If an activity is generating valuable learning but taking longer than estimated, continue it. If students grasp a concept quickly, move forward. The estimates are guides, not mandates.

Assessment Structure

Unit Exams: The Pyramid Format

The course includes 4 Unit Exams, each covering two chapters:

  • Unit A Exam: Chapters 1-2
  • Unit B Exam: Chapters 3-4
  • Unit C Exam: Chapters 5-6
  • Unit D Exam: Chapters 7-8

The Pyramid Format

Unit exams use a Pyramid format that combines individual accountability with collaborative learning:

  1. Individual Phase: Students complete the exam independently first
  2. Team Phase: Students then work in small groups to discuss and revise answers
  3. Scoring: Both individual and team scores contribute to the final grade

This format rewards both individual preparation and collaborative problem-solving—skills students will need when working with AI in their careers.

RAT: Readiness Assurance Test (Practice Exam)

Before each Unit Exam, students complete a RAT (Readiness Assurance Test)—a practice exam that:

  • Familiarizes students with the exam format and question types
  • Identifies areas needing review before the actual exam
  • Reduces test anxiety through exposure
  • Can be done the class period before the actual exam

This means each unit has 2 assessment days: one for the RAT and one for the Unit Exam.

What's NOT on exams: Content from Spotlight Essays does not appear on Unit Exams. This allows you to use Spotlights flexibly without worrying about assessment coverage.

Course Philosophy

Why Social Sciences?

Social science students are uniquely positioned to understand AI's most important dimensions:

  • Ethical implications of algorithmic decision-making
  • Social impact of AI systems on communities and democracy
  • Bias and fairness in machine learning applications
  • Human behavior in relationship to AI technology
  • Policy questions surrounding AI regulation and governance

These students may not become AI engineers, but they'll become the citizens, policymakers, journalists, educators, counselors, and community leaders who shape how AI is developed, deployed, and regulated. They need AI literacy as urgently as any computer science major—perhaps more so.

The "Alia" Approach

Throughout this course, students work with Alia—the name for our AI system customized to provide a directed and safe AI experience. Why give it a name?

  1. Demystification: A named entity feels less intimidating than "artificial intelligence system"
  2. Relationship building: Students develop a working partnership with their AI lab partner
  3. Consistency: The same system throughout ensures reliable progression of understanding
  4. Personal connection: Just as students might name a class pet, Alia becomes familiar

Students don't just chat with AI—they conduct structured experiments, test hypotheses, and document findings scientifically. This transforms passive consumption into active investigation.

The Field Journal: Heart of Scientific Learning

What is the Field Journal?

The Field Journal is where students document every lab experiment throughout the semester. Think of it as a scientist's lab notebook—a systematic record of hypotheses, observations, analyses, and insights.

Why Field Journals Matter

For students:

  • Transforms scattered AI interactions into systematic investigation
  • Develops scientific thinking and documentation skills
  • Creates study resource for assessments
  • Builds habit of reflective practice
  • Demonstrates learning progression over time

For teachers:

  • Provides ongoing formative assessment data
  • Reveals student thinking and misconceptions
  • Documents student growth across semester
  • Offers alternative assessment for diverse learners
  • Creates accountability for lab engagement

Field Journal Structure

Each lab day includes a structured journal entry with scaffolded prompts. The scaffolding gradually releases over the semester:

Phase Chapters Scaffolding Level Student Focus
Early 1-3 Highly structured Complete hypothesis frameworks provided. Specific observation prompts. Guided analysis. Focus on accurate documentation.
Middle 4-6 Moderate scaffolding Hypothesis frameworks provided, students fill specifics. Observation categories given. Students connect to broader concepts.
Later 7-8 Minimal scaffolding Students generate own hypotheses. Self-directed observation and analysis. Independent connections to real-world applications.

Grading Field Journals

Field journals are NOT graded on "right answers"—they're graded on:

Criterion Weight What to Look For
Completeness 40% All sections attempted. Evidence of effort and engagement. Timely completion.
Specificity 30% Concrete examples from experiments. Detailed observations (not vague generalizations). Accurate documentation of AI responses.
Thoughtfulness 30% Evidence of critical thinking. Connections between observations and concepts. Insightful questions and analysis.
Grading efficiency tip: Use a simple 3-point scale for each criterion: 3 = Exceeds expectations, 2 = Meets expectations, 1 = Needs improvement. Total: 9 possible points per entry. Quick calculation: (points earned / 9) × 100 = percentage grade.

The Creative Writing Project: Editor/Ghost Writer Model

Project Overview

Throughout the semester, students develop a creative writing project in collaboration with Alia using a developmental editor/ghost writer model. Students act as editors directing AI as their ghost writer—learning editorial judgment and AI direction rather than traditional writing skills.

What's a Ghost Writer?

In professional publishing, a ghost writer writes content based on someone else's ideas. Many "authored by" books are ghost-written—the credited person provides vision and direction, the ghost writer produces prose.

Student role: Developmental editor (provides vision, makes creative decisions)
Alia role: Ghost writer (generates prose based on student direction)

The Hybrid Approach

Students can:

  • Direct Alia to generate prose scenes
  • Write some prose themselves
  • Mix AI-generated and self-written content
  • Maintain editorial control throughout

Two Types of Project Time

Formal Project Days (10 total)

These days include structured activities with Alia that build specific AI collaboration skills. Students learn techniques for directing AI, evaluating output, and refining their creative vision. However, the structured activities mean less time for pure writing.

Extra Project Days (flexible)

These days provide dedicated writing time with no structured activities. Students work solely on their creative projects, applying the skills they've learned. Use these when students need uninterrupted time to develop their stories.

Students are assessed on editorial judgment and AI collaboration skills, not literary quality. The focus is on how well they direct AI, evaluate output, and maintain creative vision—not on writing talent.

Grading Structure and Components

Course Grade Composition

We recommend the semester grade be calculated from five components:

Component Weight Description
Chapter Assignments 20% Questions embedded in each lesson (8 chapter assignments total)
Unit Exams 40% End-of-unit assessments using Pyramid format (4 exams total)
Class Participation 10% Engagement during lesson and discussion days
Field Journal 15% Lab day documentation across all chapters
Creative Writing Project 15% Semester-long AI collaboration portfolio
Total 100%

Component 1: Chapter Assignments (20%)

What Is the Chapter Assignment?

A Chapter Assignment consists of questions embedded within lessons that students answer as they engage with content. These assess comprehension and engagement.

The 4-Point Rubric System

Teachers grade each question using a simple 4-point scale:

  • 1 = Needs Work: Answer shows minimal understanding or effort
  • 2 = Making Progress: Answer shows partial understanding with gaps
  • 3 = You Got It: Answer demonstrates solid understanding (this is the target/perfect score)
  • 4 = Above and Beyond: Answer shows exceptional insight or connections beyond expectations
A score of 3 ("You Got It") is considered perfect/100%. Students who earn a 3 on every question have fully mastered the material. A score of 4 is reserved for truly exceptional work that goes beyond what was asked.

Point Threshold for 100%

The point threshold for earning 100% on a Chapter Assignment = Number of Questions × 3

Example: If Chapter 2 has 40 questions:

  • Perfect score (100%) = 40 questions × 3 = 120 points
  • Student who earns all 3s = 120 points = 100%
  • Student who earns mix of 3s and 4s = more than 120 points = 100% or extra credit at your discretion

There Are 8 Chapter Assignments

Since there are 8 chapters in the course, students complete 8 Chapter Assignments over the semester.

Component 2: Unit Exams (40%)

Unit Exams are formal assessments given at the end of every two chapters (4 exams total). These use the Pyramid format combining individual and team phases.

Each exam is preceded by a RAT (Readiness Assurance Test) practice exam.

We recommend each exam counts equally toward the 40% total for this component.

Component 3: Class Participation (10%)

Class Participation includes:

  • Active engagement during Lesson Days and Discussion Days
  • Quality of contributions to class dialogue
  • Collaborative work during group activities
  • Professional conduct during Lab Days

Component 4: Field Journal (15%)

Field Journal entries document all lab experiments. Graded on completeness, specificity, and thoughtfulness—not "right answers."

See the Field Journal section above for detailed grading information.

Component 5: Creative Writing Project (15%)

Assessed on quality of AI collaboration, editorial judgment, and demonstration of AI literacy skills—NOT on literary quality.

Common Teacher Questions

"I'm not a computer science teacher. Can I really teach this?"

Yes, absolutely. This course is designed for social science teachers, not CS teachers. You don't need to understand how neural networks work at a technical level—you need to facilitate student discovery of how AI behaves and impacts society.

"What if lessons take longer than expected?"

Use your built-in flexibility. Spotlights are optional and not assessed. Extra Project Days can be added or skipped. If a lesson needs more time, take more time. Quality of learning matters more than speed through materials.

"How accurate are the time estimates?"

They're educated guesses. Time estimates in teacher guides are AI-generated approximations without direct classroom testing. Adjust based on your class's needs. Your professional judgment supersedes any estimate.

"What if students ask technical questions I can't answer?"

This is an opportunity, not a problem. Respond with: "That's a great question. Let's design an experiment to investigate it." You're teaching scientific thinking, not memorized facts.

"How much time does grading field journals take?"

With practice, expect 15-20 minutes per set of 25 journals using the rubric approach. That's roughly 30-40 seconds per entry.

"What if my school blocks Alia?"

Work with your IT department before the course starts to whitelist PocketLab.net. Explain this is an educational AI literacy course, not recreational AI use.

"Do I have to use all the Spotlight Essays?"

No. Spotlights are supplemental and their content does not appear on exams. Use them when time permits, assign them as optional enrichment, or skip them entirely if your schedule is tight.

"What's the difference between Project Days and Extra Project Days?"

Project Days include structured activities with Alia that build AI collaboration skills. Extra Project Days are dedicated writing time with no structured activities. Both are valuable—the structured days build skills, the extra days let students apply those skills with focused writing time.
